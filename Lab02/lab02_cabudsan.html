In plain words

A derivative is just a rate. The first derivative, dh/dt, is how fast the water level is changing, in metres per hour. The second derivative, d²h/dt², is whether that rate is itself speeding up or slowing down.

The peak rate from the fitted curve is 1.2295 m/h at t = 26.14 h. The finite differences give 2.5000 m/h at t = 27.00 h.

The fit, and the arithmetic behind it

Chosen model: Sum of two logistics, fitted with scipy.optimize.curve_fit(..., method="lm").

h(t) = c + a1 / (1 + exp(-k1(t - t1))) + a2 / (1 + exp(-k2(t - t2)))

Why this model

A single sigmoid cannot come back down. Two logistics have exactly two limbs, with a2 negative making the second one a recession.

Residuals

In plain words

Residuals are the misses: actual reading minus predicted value. Positive means the curve sat too low there, negative means too high.

Sign runs: The residuals switch sign only 15 times. Under random noise, we'd expect about 170 switches. This pattern means the model is missing some real structure in the data.

Verdict. R² = 0.998660 and s = 0.0972 m. Trust this fit for volumes and timing to about a decimetre. Do not trust it for the level at an instant to the centimetre.

In plain words

Integrating means measuring the area between the curve and the bottom axis. Here the curve is water level and the axis is time, so the area adds up "how high, for how long" across the whole three days.

The area is 1285.6 metre-hours. Divide by the 71.75 h record and you get an average level of 17.9182 m.

It is not a volume of water. The sensor measures depth at one point; it knows nothing about how wide the reservoir is at any height.

Units
A = 1285.6296 m·h. Not a volume - that needs surface area as a function of stage, which a depth sensor does not measure.
